Extraction of Indian Cultural Elements Using RAG

Which plants and animals live in eight Indian books, what a small language model said was there, and why the two lists do not match.

Simanta Nandi  /  MA, Digital Humanities and Social Sciences, IIT (ISM) Dhanbad

Move the lamp across the dark to read. Under it, every name of a plant or animal glows.

Abstract

This project asks a plain question. When a small language model reads Indian literature through a retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipeline, can it tell us which plants and animals the texts name?

I built a repository of eight Indian literary texts in English: six public-domain files from Project Gutenberg, Naidu's The Bird of Time from Wikisource, and Hansda Sowvendra Shekhar's The Adivasi Will Not Dance, which is still in copyright. Each one went into AnythingLLM and was queried with Google's Gemma 3 4B, running on my own machine through LM Studio. The prompt asked for every tree, plant, flower, herb and crop, and every mammal, bird, reptile, fish and insect, each backed by an exact quotation.

The answers looked careful, but most were not right. Of the 106 rows the model returned, only 19 were fully correct. Many of its quotations do not exist in the books. Some of its "animals" were people, a passenger jeep and chewing tobacco. To find out what the texts really contain, I read every candidate name against the full text of all eight books, using WordNet only to suggest candidates. The result is 509 text-level entries (469 verified and 40 ambiguous). Each is tied to a quotation that a script copied from the source and then re-checked word for word.

The distance between the two lists is the main finding. It opens up in four places. Retrieval showed the model four chunks of each book, which is between 1 and 7 per cent of it. The model and the lexical tools around it were shaped mostly by Western data. The colonial English of the translations had already swapped some Indian birds and trees for European ones. And the ten-category scheme fits Indian ritual plants badly. This site lists every name text by text and sets out where each kind of bias comes in.

Keywords: retrieval-augmented generation, AnythingLLM, Gemma 3, ecocriticism, Indian English literature, translation, flora and fauna, hallucination, algorithmic bias, digital humanities

Project objectives

  • Build a small, documented repository of Indian literary texts in English, with clean plain-text copies and full citations.
  • Run the RAG pipeline shown in class (AnythingLLM with Gemma 3 4B in LM Studio) on each text, using one detailed extraction prompt.
  • Extract the names of flora and fauna under the ten required categories, and keep each Indian-language name in the form the text uses.
  • Check every answer the model gave against the full source, and record what it got right, half right or wrong.
  • Describe the kinds of bias that shape both the model's answers and the texts themselves.
  • Publish the results as a Python file, flora_fauna.py, and as this website.

Research questions

  1. RQ1 How many names of plants and animals do these texts contain, and how are they spread across the ten categories?
  2. RQ2 How much of that list does a small RAG pipeline recover, and how often are its quotations real?
  3. RQ3 Where does the pipeline fail: in retrieval, in the model, or in the texts and their translations?
  4. RQ4 Which Indian names survive the move into English, and which are replaced by European ones?

The repository of texts

The eight texts were chosen in class, and all eight were checked against the full text. Six are plain-text files from Project Gutenberg. I removed the Gutenberg header and licence and kept everything else, including introductions and translators' notes, because AnythingLLM was given those too. Line numbers on this site refer to these cleaned files, which sit in the repository beside this page.

The Bird of Time (Sarojini Naidu, 1912) is not on Project Gutenberg. Its text comes from the full-text edition in Literature of Colonial South Asia, a digital archive edited by Amardeep Singh at Lehigh University, which reproduces the Wikisource transcription with Edmund Gosse's introduction. The Adivasi Will Not Dance (Hansda Sowvendra Shekhar, 2015) is still in copyright. I worked from a text file supplied for the project. That file is not in the repository, and the site quotes only the short passages needed to show each name.

Method

The RAG pipeline demonstrated in class was run as it was set up in AnythingLLM. Its answers were then checked against the full text of each book, name by name, and every quotation on this site was copied from the source by a script and compared with it word for word.

The RAG pipeline in AnythingLLM

  1. UploadEach book was put into its own AnythingLLM workspace as a Gutenberg .txt file.
  2. ChunkAnythingLLM cut the file into pieces of about 1,000 characters.
  3. Embed and storeEach piece became a vector in the workspace's vector database.
  4. RetrieveThe prompt was turned into a vector, and the 4 closest pieces with a similarity of at least 0.25 were kept.
  5. GenerateGemma 3 4B in LM Studio read those 4 pieces with the prompt, at temperature 0.1.
  6. AnswerThe model returned two tables, one for flora and one for fauna, with quotations.

The extraction prompt

One long prompt was used for every book. It sets a research-assistant role, defines the ten categories with examples, demands an exact quotation and location for each name, asks the model to keep Indian-language spellings, to mark doubtful names as ambiguous, to keep generic words apart, and to say honestly how much of the book it saw. It ends with a quality-control checklist. On paper it is a strict prompt. The audit below shows how little a prompt can do when the evidence never reaches the model.

Rules for what counts

I followed the prompt's own rules and wrote down every judgement call. Generic words such as tree, flower, bird or fish are listed separately and not counted. Plant parts (buds, petals, leaves, thorns) are left out. A character's name is not counted on its own, but the animal is: Shere Khan is counted under tiger, and the remark gives his name. A product is counted when its source is clear (sandalwood, cinnamon, betel-nut), and the remark says it is a product. Colour words such as "saffron footcloths" or "the raven hair" are rejected. Mythical beings (the makara, Shesha, the trees of Indra's garden, Naidu's jewelled serpent-king) are rejected, and so are brand names such as Kingfisher beer and Rajnigandha pan masala. Conches, clams, frogs and worms are real animals but fall outside the ten categories, so they are recorded under a separate heading. Names that appear only in an introduction or a translator's note are kept but flagged as paratext.

Auditing the AnythingLLM answers

Each row the model returned was checked against the full text and given one verdict. Correct means a real name, a sensible category and a genuine quotation that supports it. Partly correct means the name is real but something else is wrong: the quotation, the category, the location or the identification. Fabricated or unsupported means the name or the quotation is not in the book, or no evidence was given. Not flora or fauna means a person, place, object or plant part was filed as an organism. Generic means a word like "trees" or "insects" was given as if it were a species.

Seven moments from the audit

Gitanjali: deer that were never there

"The deer graze peacefully in the meadow." Poem II.5, according to the model

There are no deer or cattle anywhere in Gitanjali, and none of the model's fifteen quotations occurs in the book. The workspace held 2 vectors instead of about 98, so the model had almost nothing to read and wrote a plausible nature poem instead. Deer and cows are both examples in the prompt.

Toru Dutt: the silk-cotton tree read as a creeper

"Seemuls: Plant (Uncertain, likely a local vine/creeper)"

The quotation was real: "o'er the quiet pools the seemuls lean, / Red,--red, and startling like a trumpet's sound." The seemul is the Bengali shimul, the silk-cotton tree, whose red flowers open on bare branches. The model could not place a Bengali tree name and guessed.

The Jungle Book: chapters that do not exist

"Chil / Rann ... Brahminy Kite (Haliaeetus leucoryphus) ... The Fall Wind"

"The Spring Hunts", "The Fall Wind" and "Mogli Can See" are not chapters of the book. Chil belongs to The Second Jungle Book. The Latin name is Pallas's fish eagle, and "Ursus sloth" is not a species at all. The dhak tree is in the text, one line away from the quotation the model chose.

Sarojini Naidu: the koel becomes a weaverbird

"Kokila: Bird, Weaverbird"

The line "A kokila called from a henna-spray" is genuine. But the kokila is the Asian koel, a cuckoo and the most familiar songbird in Indian poetry. The model also "implied" sharks and leaping fish in "Coromandel Fishers". Neither is in the poem.

Sarojini Naidu again: Gosse's robins

"Robins: Flower ... Skylarks: Flower"

For The Bird of Time every quotation the model gave comes from Edmund Gosse's introduction, not from the poems. That is where Gosse tells the young Naidu to "write no more about robins and skylarks". The model listed both as flowers. The poems themselves name the koel, the papeeha, the bulbul and the champak, and not one of the nineteen rows was fully correct.

The Adivasi Will Not Dance: people as animals

"villagers: Mammals ... cricketers: Birds ... gutkha: Insects"

The quotations are all genuine, but every row is a category error. A "treker" (a shared passenger jeep) became a mammal, the word Adivasi became a herb, and chewing tobacco became an insect. The stories name sal, mahua, tendu, the Santali matkom, the krait, rui and katla. None of them reached the table.

Sakoontala: a river taken for a tree

"Máliní: Trees, Willow" and "No mammal references found."

The Máliní is the river beside Kanwa's hermitage. The play opens with the king chasing an antelope, and the fauna table says there are no mammals, birds, reptiles, fish or insects at all. The asoka was glossed as the Bodhi tree.

Bias: where the Indian names get lost

Models like Gemma learn from web-scale data, and on the web English and Western sources far outnumber Indian ones. The books here are Indian. That mismatch matters, but it is not the only source of bias. Bias enters at several points before the model ever answers, and some of it was already in the books a century ago.

1. The model's training data

Google has not published a full breakdown of Gemma 3's training data, so I cannot measure this directly. The errors, though, lean one way. When the model met a name it did not know, it reached for a Western or pan-Asian default. The kokila became a "Weaverbird". The asoka became the "Bodhi Tree". The seemul became "a local vine". The Máliní river became a willow. The unidentified dhadikulas became "Songbirds/Larks". In Gitanjali, with no text to read, it produced swans and birds of paradise, the stock birds of English nature writing. Not one of its guesses went the other way, towards an Indian name.

2. The lexical tools

I used WordNet, the standard English lexical database, to find candidates. It knows banyan, peepul, neem, palmyra and betel. It does not know kokila, koel, seemul, babla, tulsi, asoka, champak, sirish, sal, sambhur, mahseer, kusha, keora, kadam, amla or papiya. It read "paddy" (as in "paddy birds", the pond heron) as a grain and "Nag" as a horse. A pipeline that relies on such a resource without a second net will lose the Indian names first. I caught them only because I listed the words WordNet did not know and read them one by one.

3. The English of the books

Some of the bias was in the texts long before any model. Tagore's own translation of Gitanjali gives "the cuckoo" where the Bengali bird is almost certainly the koel. Toru Dutt fills a Bengal forest with nightingales, rooks and a roebuck, none of which lives there, and calls a langur in her garden a "grey baboon". Kipling calls the musk shrew, chuchundar, a "musk-rat". A Bombay playbill of 1855, quoted in Monier-Williams's introduction, glosses the black bee, bhramar, as "(fly)". Monier-Williams measures the asoka against "an ordinary cherry-tree", likens the Indian roller to "our jay", and gives the tree as Jonesia Asoka, a genus named after the British orientalist Sir William Jones. The "our" in "our jay" is a European reader. A model trained on English inherits that point of view, and the books hand it over again.

4. Retrieval and the prompt

With four chunks, a model sees between one and seven per cent of a book (four out of the 60 to 323 vectors in the workspaces). Everything else is invisible to it, and a strict prompt cannot change that. The prompt itself also nudged the answers. Its examples (banyan, peepal, neem, mango, sal, mahua, coconut, bamboo; elephant, tiger, deer, cow) were meant as illustrations. But the hallucinated rows match them closely: deer and cattle in Gitanjali, a sal tree in The Jungle Book, an "implied" deer in Toru Dutt. When the evidence is thin, examples in a prompt become answers.

5. The categories

The ten categories follow European natural history. They work well for mammals and birds. They work badly for Indian ritual plants. Across eight books only four names fit "Herbs": tulsi, kusha grass, usira (vetiver) and Naidu's "basil-wreath", which may or may not be tulsi. Kusha is a grass and a sacred object at once. The conch, which sounds through Gitanjali, Toru Dutt, Tagore's letters and Kipling, is not a plant or one of the five animal classes, so the scheme has nowhere to put it. Where a category is missing, the culture it carries goes missing too.

6. The archive

The corpus is all in English. Three of the eight texts come from Bengal's educated elite (two by Tagore, one by Toru Dutt), and two are by Naidu. One is by a British imperial writer, and one is a Sanskrit play in a Victorian scholar's translation. The only text by an Adivasi writer is the only one still in copyright. It could be checked here because a copy was supplied, but it cannot sit in the repository beside the others. It is also the text with the highest share of Indian-language names (31 per cent), and the one where the model found no real plant or animal at all. Free archives hold what is old enough to be free, and that means colonial-era English. A study built on them will keep finding the colonial voice, unless other voices are added on purpose.

7. Setting

Not everything in an "Indian" book is Indian. Two stories in The Jungle Book are set in the Bering Sea, so 27 of its rows (seals, walrus, halibut, puffins) are tagged as non-Indian or mixed. Naidu's Persian court poems bring hyacinths, tulips, orioles and peach groves, and Gosse's introduction to The Bird of Time brings English robins, skylarks and a lily of the valley. Tagore's letters drift into an Arabian Nights daydream of date groves and nightingales. The explorer lets you filter these out.

8. My own choices

The checking track has its own bias, and it is mine. I decided what counts as generic, which senses to exclude and which names to merge. The English and Latin names in the second column come from standard botany and zoology, not from the texts. That breaks the prompt's rule against outside knowledge, so those names are kept in a separate column and every doubtful one is marked ambiguous. A second reader would make some different calls.

Analysis and discussion

What the books contain

The Jungle Book (99 entries), Toru Dutt's Ancient Ballads (82), Sakoontala (72) and The Bird of Time (70) are the richest. Gitanjali is the sparest, with 18. Its world is mostly light, sky and song, and the few named plants (the banyan, the neem, the babla) appear at the edges of a single afternoon in Poem 54.

Each book has its own ecology. Tagore's letters from the family estates are the most agricultural: rice sown on silt, jute carried to market, sugar-cane along the Ichamati, and buffaloes in the shallows. They show rural Bengal from a landlord's houseboat. Toru Dutt's poems "Buttoo" and "Baugmaree" read like a catalogue of a Bengal garden, with sal, tamarind, neem, seemul, champak, nagessur and sirish set side by side. She writes them in Bengali forms, then lets English stock birds into the same lines. Naidu's Hyderabad mixes the Indian (champa, koil, neem, henna) with the Persian (hyacinth, tulip, nightingale). In The Bird of Time she goes further into Hindustani names, the koel, the papeeha and the gulmohur, and glosses the papeeha's call herself. Shekhar's Santhal world is a working one: sal and tendu leaves and mahua blossoms gathered for the market, rice planted by migrant families on zamindars' farms in Bardhaman, rui and katla haggled over at the fish stall, and silver carp marinated with turmeric. In Sakoontala plants and animals are kin. The heroine says goodbye to her jasmine and her pet fawn as if to sisters, and the sacred kusha grass marks every ritual.

Kipling is a special case. His animals carry Hindi names as personal names: Baloo (bhalu, bear), Hathi (elephant), the Bandar-log (monkey people), Nag (cobra), Darzee (tailor) and Chuchundra (musk shrew). The Indian word survives, but as a character's name rather than as the ordinary name of a species. That is why The Jungle Book has the lowest share of Indian-language forms, 10 per cent. The Adivasi Will Not Dance has the highest, 31 per cent, followed by Sakoontala at 26.

What the pipeline did

Across the eight texts, the model returned 67 rows that hold a real name, against 469 verified entries. When the retrieved chunks happened to hold names, it did reasonably well: seven correct rows each for Toru Dutt's sonnets and Tagore's letters. When they did not, it filled the gap. It did so politely and in the exact format the prompt asked for, with section numbers, quotation marks and a note on limitations. That polish is the danger. An answer that looks like a careful scholar's work is easy to trust and hard to check.

Most of the failure starts before generation. The Gitanjali workspace barely held the book. The other workspaces held it, but showed the model four chunks out of 60 to 323. A larger model would quote more accurately from the same four chunks. It would still not see the remaining 93 to 99 per cent of the book.

What would make it better

Three changes would help most. First, query each category separately, so that the pipeline retrieves many small, targeted sets of chunks rather than one. Second, add a lexicon of Indian names (Bengali, Hindi, Sanskrit and Anglo-Indian) to the search, so that kokila and seemul are looked for on purpose. Third, never accept a quotation without checking it against the source. rag_pipeline.py includes that check, and it would have flagged every invented quotation in this audit within seconds.

Limitations

  • The Adivasi Will Not Dance is in copyright. Its file is not shared, so readers cannot check its line numbers from this site, only its quotations against a printed copy.
  • The Bird of Time was checked against a Wikisource transcription, not the 1912 printing. Small differences of spelling or punctuation are possible.
  • All texts are English versions. I did not compare the Bengali or Sanskrit originals, so I cannot say what the translators changed in every case.
  • One person did the checking. There is no second annotator and no agreement score.
  • Each book was queried once. Language models vary from run to run, and I did not repeat the queries.
  • Plain-text files have no page numbers, so locations are given by section and line number in the cleaned files.
  • WordNet and my word lists may still have missed a name. A rare word that is also a common English word could slip through both nets.
  • The line between a generic word and a named kind (grass, palm, snake) is a judgement call, and other readers might draw it elsewhere.

Conclusion

The eight books are full of Indian plants and animals, named in Bengali, Sanskrit, Hindi, Persian and colonial English. A small RAG pipeline, run with a strict prompt, found about one name in seven. It also invented quotations, mislabelled Indian species with Western ones, and filed people and products as animals.

The fault is not only the model's. Retrieval showed it too little of each book. The lexical tools did not know the Indian names. Some translations had already changed them, and the categories had no place for ritual plants and shells. For Indian cultural material, a RAG answer is a set of leads to check, not a result. The verified lists on this site, and the quotations behind them, are the result.
